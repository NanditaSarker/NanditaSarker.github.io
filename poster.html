Source-specific contributions to polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon exposure in the U.S.: tobacco, dietary, and occupational sources (NHANES 2001–2014)

Nandita Sarker1 and Garett Sansom1

1Department of Environmental and Occupational Health, School of Public Health, Texas A&M University, College Station, TX 77843, USA

This page is the accessible text version of my American Public Health Association (APHA) 2026 Annual Meeting and Expo presentation in San Antonio.

Key findings

  • Active smokers had up to 6.8 times higher PAH levels than non-exposed adults.
  • Diet and occupation each explained less than 2% of the differences in PAH levels.

Background

Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) are widespread environmental pollutants generated from incomplete combustion. In the U.S., major exposure sources include tobacco smoke, diet, and occupational environments. However, their relative contributions to internal dose and the role of unidentified sources remain unclear.

Methods

We analyzed NHANES 2001–2014 data among 4,887 U.S. adults (20–65 years). Creatinine-adjusted urinary PAH metabolites (naphthalene, fluorene, phenanthrene, pyrene), serum cotinine, dietary intake, occupation, and demographic variables were included. Survey-weighted regression estimated adjusted geometric means (GMs) and geometric mean ratios (GMRs). Hierarchical nested models quantified source contributions using incremental R2 (ΔR2).

Results

Naphthalene concentrations were highest across metabolites. Tobacco smoke exposure showed a strong dose–response relationship across all PAH metabolites. Compared with non-exposed individuals (<0.015 ng/mL cotinine), active smokers (>15 ng/mL) had substantially elevated adjusted geometric means (all p<0.0001). The geometric mean ratios (GMRs) were:

  • Fluorene: GMR = 6.76 (95% CI: 6.25–7.30)
  • Naphthalene: GMR = 3.87 (95% CI: 3.50–4.27)
  • Phenanthrene: GMR = 2.17 (95% CI: 2.02–2.33)
  • Pyrene: GMR = 2.53 (95% CI: 2.30–2.79)

Production, construction, and repair workers had higher levels of fluorene (GMR = 1.14, p = 0.002) and naphthalene (GMR = 1.10, p = 0.047) than management and professional occupations.

Demographic factors alone explained only 4.6–8.0% of the variability in PAH metabolites. Adding tobacco exposure dramatically increased explained variance (R2, the share of variability explained, before and after adding tobacco exposure):

  • Fluorene: R2 increased from 0.080 to 0.546 (ΔR2 = 0.466)
  • Naphthalene: from 0.048 to 0.305 (ΔR2 = 0.257)
  • Phenanthrene: from 0.047 to 0.185 (ΔR2 = 0.139)
  • Pyrene: from 0.047 to 0.189 (ΔR2 = 0.143)

In contrast, diet and occupation contributed minimally to incremental variance (less than 2% and no more than 1.6%, respectively). This indicates potential contributions from unmeasured or unidentified sources.

Conclusions

Tobacco smoke is the largest contributor to PAH body burden in this nationally representative sample. These findings highlight tobacco control and targeted occupational protections as key strategies to reduce exposure. This study also provides a baseline for future research to identify additional sources and evaluate the impact of emerging policies and interventions on PAH exposure in the U.S.
